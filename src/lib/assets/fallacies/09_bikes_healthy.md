# Bikes Are Only For Young Healthy People
👴
Not everyone can ride a bike! What about elderly people, disabled people, or parents with kids?

## Response

This describes the inadequacy of current infrastructure, not the limitations of cycling. In the Netherlands, 25% of trips by people over 65 are by bike, and cargo bikes routinely transport multiple children. E-bikes make cycling accessible to people with limited strength or mobility. Protected bike lanes that feel safe for an 8-year-old or an 80-year-old open cycling to everyone. For those who truly cannot bike, good bike infrastructure correlates with good pedestrian infrastructure and transit service. The real accessibility issue is car dependency, which excludes children, many elderly people, disabled people without specialized vehicles, and people who can't afford $10,000/year in car costs. Multi-modal cities with walking, cycling, and transit infrastructure are far more accessible than car-dependent sprawl.

E-bikes have revolutionized cycling accessibility, making it practical for people with physical limitations, longer commutes, or hilly terrain. Research shows e-bike adoption is highest among people over 55 and those with mobility challenges. The electric assist allows riders to maintain comfortable speeds while choosing their level of exertion. Cities seeing rapid e-bike growth report that new riders include many people who hadn't biked in decades but find e-bikes practical for commuting and errands. For cargo transport, electric cargo bikes can carry 200+ pounds of children and groceries while remaining easy to pedal, making them viable car replacements for families.

The accessibility argument also ignores that car dependency creates the most exclusionary transportation system. Children under 16 can't drive and are often too far from destinations to walk or bike safely. People with visual impairments, seizure disorders, or other conditions that prevent driving are effectively immobilized in car-dependent areas. Low-income households spend 20-30% of income on mandatory car ownership, forcing impossible tradeoffs with food, housing, and healthcare. Meanwhile, cities with robust cycling, walking, and transit infrastructure serve all ages and abilities. An 8-year-old can bike to school, an 85-year-old can take the bus to the doctor, and a person using a wheelchair can access wide sidewalks and level-boarding transit - none of which require expensive specialized equipment or vehicle modifications.
